PAPER REPORT
Title: Verify and Trust: A Multidimensional Survey of Zero-Trust Security in the Age of IoT
Authors: Muhammad Ajmal Azad, Sidrah Abdullah, Junaid Arshad, Harjinder Lallie, Yussuf Hassan Ahmed
Journal: Internet of Things, Vol. 27 (2024), Article 101227
Publisher: Elsevier

===================================================================
1. ABSTRACT
===================================================================
The paper surveys the Zero-Trust (ZT) security model, which assumes no user,
device, or network traffic should be trusted until proven otherwise. It
explains that access is granted only after successful authentication of
credentials and evaluation of factors such as user identity, device health,
location, and behaviour, with continuous re-evaluation as these properties
change. The authors argue ZT can be applied across domains (healthcare,
manufacturing, finance, government) to reduce risk and protect critical
assets. The paper aims to give an in-depth analysis of ZT principles,
components, and practical applications, and to provide recommendations for
organizations adopting it, along with open research issues around security
and privacy.

===================================================================
2. INTRODUCTION
===================================================================
The introduction motivates ZT by citing the explosive growth of IoT devices
(41+ billion devices), rising data breach costs ($4.45 million average in
2023, a 15% increase over three years), and projected cybercrime costs of
$10.5 trillion by 2025. It contrasts traditional perimeter-based security
(which trusts everything inside the network once access is granted) with
Zero Trust, which continuously verifies and authenticates users, devices,
and applications regardless of location. The authors note that implementing
ZT in heterogeneous, BYOD-heavy networks is challenging due to complexity,
interoperability, cost, and user experience issues. They state this is the
first attempt at a comprehensive survey of ZT across authentication,
authorization, architecture, and application domains, and align their
review with the MITRE ATT&CK framework.

Paper Selection Methodology:
- Databases searched: ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, Science Direct,
  Scopus, and Google Scholar.
- Initial queries: "zero trust architecture," "zero trust models," "zero
  trust frameworks," followed by advanced queries on security aspects,
  segmentation, authentication/authorization, IoT/cloud, and blockchain.
- Inclusion criteria: peer-reviewed papers (2010 onward) focused on security
  aspects of ZTA (architecture, implementation, or technology).
- Exclusion criteria: papers describing only commercial ZTA variants, papers
  lacking functional component definitions, non-English papers.
- Search yielded 24 (ACM), 36 (Science Direct/Scopus), 110 (IEEE), 415
  (Google Scholar) articles; after exclusion, 8, 13, 31, and 20 papers
  respectively were retained (72 total papers analyzed).
- Papers were grouped into: architecture (cloud-based: 19, IoT-based: 20,
  blockchain-based: 12 contributions), authentication (7) and authorization
  (12), and implementation domains.

===================================================================
3. BACKGROUND
===================================================================
Importance of Zero-Trust: ZT assumes all traffic, devices, and users are
potentially malicious, enforcing least-privilege access and continuous
verification. The paper contrasts this with perimeter security (firewalls,
IDS/IPS, VPNs) which trusts insiders — a major weakness given that insider
attacks rose ~47% (2022-2024) and cost an average of $15.38 million per
incident in 2023.

Requirements for Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA), the paper identifies five
essential components:
1. Identity Authentication - verifying users/devices/processes via
   passwords, MFA, IAM, PKI, biometrics, and behavioural profiling.
2. Network Segmentation - dividing the network into micro-segments/zones
   protected by firewalls and access controls to limit lateral movement;
   can be implemented via SDN.
3. Authorization - enforced via ACLs, RBAC, ABAC, or policy-based access
   control (PBAC), applying least privilege dynamically.
4. Encryption - protecting data at rest, in transit, and in use, using
   end-to-end or point-to-point encryption; lightweight cryptography is
   needed for constrained IoT devices.
5. Network Monitoring - continuous collection/analysis of logs and traffic
   using ML/AI and IDS/IPS deployed at network, edge, or device layers.

Components of Zero-Trust Architecture (NIST model): The paper describes
three logical components:
- Policy Decision Point (PDP): makes access/authorization decisions based
  on policy rules, contextual data, and risk factors; should be
  decentralized/redundant to avoid single point of failure.
- Policy Enforcement Point (PEP): enforces the PDP's decisions; should be
  located near the resource/user to minimize latency.
- Policy Administrator (PA): gatekeeper managing connections, issuing
  authentication tokens, and enforcing policy at network/application
  levels.

How Zero Trust Differs from Traditional Security: Perimeter-based security
(Fig. 5 in paper) uses firewalls, IDS, and DMZs to protect from outsiders
but trusts insiders — a flaw since insiders cause ~30% of breaches
(~$11.45 million average loss). Perimeter security is reactive, inflexible
to evolving threats (e.g., low-rate DoS/DDoS), and doesn't scale well with
BYOD/IoT/cloud. ZTA (Fig. 6) treats all devices/users/traffic as untrusted
regardless of origin, using MFA, ABAC/RBAC/PBAC, and continuous monitoring.

5. LITERATURE REVIEW
===================================================================
The paper reviews and tabulates (Table 2) around 60+ prior works across
three categories:

Cloud-based ZT systems: e.g., Decusatis et al. (transport-level access
control with first-packet authentication), Hussain et al. (Intelligent
Service Mesh with ML/AI), Ferretti et al. (survivable ZT with trust engine
and fault-tolerant controller), Zaheer et al. (ezTrust — network-independent
microservice protection), Mehraj & Banday (segmentation + ML threat
detection), Eidle et al. (autonomic security framework), Sheikh et al. and
others (micro-segmentation approaches).

IoT-based ZT systems: e.g., Wenhua Huang et al. (ZT-Access using
attribute-based encryption for power IoT), Samaniego & Deters (blockchain
hierarchical ZT management), Zhang Xiaojian et al. (power IoT ZT
framework), Anil G et al. (lightweight crypto + ML intrusion detection for
Enterprise IoT), Ameer et al. (score-based authorization), Dimitrakos et
al. (trust-aware continuous authorization using UCON/ABAC).

Decentralized and blockchain-based ZT systems: e.g., Li et al. (EV charger
ZT security with blockchain key management), Han et al. (6G edge IoT ZT
with blockchain), Mukesh Kumar et al. (blockchain group authentication for
6G), Patil et al. (consensus algorithm for decentralized ZT), Dhar & Bose
(risk score with blockchain-based access control), Wu et al. and Gai Keke
et al. (attribute-based access control via blockchain with homomorphic
cryptosystem).

The literature review is also organized around MITRE ATT&CK alignment
(Section 3), authentication and authorization mechanisms (Section 6), and
implementation domains such as healthcare, wireless networks, and
enterprise networks (Section 6.1-6.3, referenced but not detailed in the
provided excerpt).

--- 6.2 Methods Discussed ---
Implementation models for ZTA (Section 4):
- Centralized Implementation: single PDP/PE/PEP for entire organization;
  easy to manage but single point of failure/attack, poor scalability,
  cannot support peer-to-peer/ad-hoc networks.
- Distributed Implementation: each network segment has its own PDP/PEP/PE;
  resilient to segment failure, reduces attack surface, but requires
  careful policy consistency planning and more resources.
- Decentralized Implementation: functionality distributed across network
  (e.g., policy definition centralized but enforcement near edge/fog
  device); reduces attack surface and single point of failure but has
  management overhead; can use SDN/NFV.
- Blockchain-based Implementation: PDP/PEP implemented via smart
  contracts on distributed ledger; provides tamper-proof records of
  access/authorization events, enhanced security/decentralization, at the
  cost of complexity and computational overhead.
- Cloud-based Implementation: ZTA components hosted in cloud (PaaS/SaaS
  used for PDP/PEP functions); offers scalability, resilience,
  cost-effectiveness (e.g., AWS, Azure, Google Cloud for policy/access
  management).

Authorization mechanisms discussed: DAC, MAC, RBAC, ABAC, PBAC,
Identity-Based Access Control (IBAC), Risk-Based Access Control, and
score-based/context-aware authorization.

Authentication mechanisms discussed: password-based, MFA, biometrics,
continuous/behavioural authentication, blockchain-based authentication
(eliminating central authority), pseudonymous authentication protocols,
and secret-sharing-based continuous authentication without a trusted
authority.

--- 6.3 System Requirements (Essential ZTA Components) ---
1. Identity Authentication mechanisms (password, MFA, IAM, PKI, biometrics)
2. Network Segmentation / micro-segmentation infrastructure
3. Authorization engine (ACL/RBAC/ABAC/PBAC)
4. Encryption (data at rest, in transit, in use; lightweight crypto for IoT)
5. Continuous Network Monitoring (ML/AI, IDS/IPS, SIEM)
6. Logical components: Policy Decision Point, Policy Enforcement Point,
   Policy Administrator
7. Supporting components: Continuous Diagnostics & Mitigation (CDM),
   Industry Compliance modules, Threat Intelligence feeds, Activity Logs,
   PKI infrastructure, Identity Management, SIEM systems

7. RESULTS
===================================================================
The paper is a survey (no experimental results/numeric performance data),
but its key findings/conclusions include:
- ZT reduces attack footprint by enforcing least-privilege access and
  continuous verification, aligning with most MITRE ATT&CK tactic
  categories (Initial Access, Persistence, Privilege Escalation,
  Credential Access, Command & Control, Defense Evasion, Impact, Lateral
  Movement) — micro-segmentation and continuous monitoring specifically
  limit initial access impact and lateral movement.
- The majority of surveyed ZT systems are cloud-based, split between
  centralized cloud (simple, stable, but higher latency/resource needs)
  and edge-based decentralized (lower latency, more complex management,
  but more secure via smaller segments).
- Distributed and blockchain-based systems show promise for
  resource-constrained/decentralized networks but require further work on
  deployment overhead, communication cost, and consensus mechanisms.
- RBAC works well for static networks; PBAC/ABAC perform better in dynamic,
  heterogeneous environments but are more complex to manage.
- Table 2 comparison shows most existing works implement only a subset of
  desirable properties (authorization, authentication, access control,
  cryptography, MFA, network segmentation) — very few papers achieve all
  six simultaneously.

===================================================================
8. FUTURE DIRECTIONS
===================================================================
The paper identifies several open research challenges (Section 8):
1. Segmentation: difficulty integrating micro-segmentation with legacy
   systems, heterogeneous vendor protocols, and resource-constrained
   devices; need lightweight segmentation protocols.
2. Scalability: ZTA must scale with growing users/devices/data in a
   plug-and-play manner; needs research into ML/AI-driven automated
   policy management and SDN/NFV/cloud-based scalability solutions.
3. Decentralized Implementation: managing complexity, data privacy, and
   consensus algorithm overhead (especially for blockchain-based ZT) in
   decentralized/distributed setups.
4. Interoperability: lack of standard protocols across ZT vendors and
   networks; need agreed standards for heterogeneous device communication.
5. Machine Learning and AI: need lightweight, explainable, and
   privacy-preserving ML/AI (e.g., federated learning) for anomaly
   detection without exposing private micro-segment data.
6. Incident Response: micro-segmentation can reduce visibility into
   network-wide attacks; high volume of ZT control traffic complicates
   event correlation across heterogeneous devices.
7. End-to-end encryption/key management across multiple segments and
   diverse devices; Attribute-Based Encryption (ABE) is promising but
   complex to scale.

===================================================================
9. CONCLUSION
===================================================================
Zero Trust assumes no inherent trust for any user, device, process, or
application; all traffic must be authenticated, authorized, and verified
before resource access is granted. The authors systematically reviewed
ZTA across three dimensions — architectural setup, authorization/
authentication mechanisms, and MITRE ATT&CK alignment — and examined
cloud-based, IoT-based, and blockchain-based implementations along with
their security/privacy properties. They conclude that implementing ZT in
continuously changing, resource-intensive environments is challenging
(resources, training, implementation scenarios), but that ZT is likely to
reduce zero-day attacks and minimize attacker footprint through
micro-segmentation. Organizations should carefully evaluate network needs,
budget, and human resource constraints before adopting Zero Trust.

===================================================================
RELEVANCE TO "ZTA FOR WSN" TOPIC
===================================================================
This survey provides the foundational vocabulary and framework (PDP/PEP/PA,
authentication/authorization models, micro-segmentation, MITRE ATT&CK
mapping) needed to frame Zero Trust for Wireless Sensor Networks. Its
discussion of IoT-based ZT implementations, lightweight cryptography
requirements for resource-constrained devices, and network segmentation
strategies for the "device, fog, or edge level" are directly applicable
to WSN nodes, which are similarly resource-constrained, distributed, and
prone to insider/physical-layer threats.
